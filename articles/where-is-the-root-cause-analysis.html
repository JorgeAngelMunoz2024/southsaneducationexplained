Where Is the Root-Cause Analysis?

Were Teachers Really What South San Needed to Fix? — Part 3

Published: September 2026

New to the series? Read Part 1 and Part 2.

In Part 2, we followed the public record through the April 14, 2025 vote to approve districtwide NIET/TAP. South San’s earlier Needs Assessment had not identified deficiencies in teaching practice as a districtwide root cause of low student performance, and a new Needs Assessment was still to come. But the story did not end with the April 14 vote.

What Happened After April 14?

What the Timeline Shows

The timeline picks up where Part 2 left off. On April 14, the Board approved districtwide NIET/TAP while the new Needs Assessment Saavedra had described was still to come. By August, South San’s Draft Implementation Plan listed “Administration Data Analysis & Root Cause Recommendations.”

Then, on August 25...

The Board met for a workshop where Hinojosa and his team presented what was described as their “data analysis and root cause.”

Could this be the new Needs Assessment Saavedra had told the Board about in April?

Recall that on April 14, TEA Conservator Abe Saavedra said he, Hinojosa, and district staff would conduct a new Needs Assessment over the following several weeks and present it to the Board. Yet the Board approved districtwide NIET/TAP that same night, before the new assessment had been completed. As we saw in Part 2, the public materials for that vote did not include South San-specific data or analysis showing that deficiencies in teaching practice were a significant cause of low student performance.

By then, NIET/TAP had already restructured classroom instruction and changed how teachers were expected to teach, as well as how they were coached, observed, and evaluated. If the August presentation was the promised Needs Assessment—or the root-cause analysis that grew out of it—was this when the public would finally see the evidence behind that already implemented decision?

During the August 25 workshop, Hinojosa asked for a “PowerPoint presentation,” and participants referred to and moved among slides while the administration presented its “data analysis and root cause.” This sounded like material that might answer questions left unresolved after the April vote.

But there is a problem: the presentation they were looking at is not shown in the public meeting video we reviewed. We can hear participants referring to and moving between the slides, but the viewer never gets to see them. Read the video transcript here. We also did not locate the presentation among the publicly posted workshop materials.

So what was in the presentation the Board was looking at—and was this the Needs Assessment Saavedra said at the April 14 meeting would later be presented to them?

In late August 2026, South San AFT—the local affiliate of the American Federation of Teachers—submitted a public-records request for “a copy of the PowerPoint on Data Analysis and Root Causes presented by Dr. Hinojosa at the August 25, 2025 Board of Managers special session.”

The district responded by sending the same two documents already publicly posted for the workshop—the Draft Implementation Plan and Corrective Action Plan/Exit Criteria—and stated: “There were no PowerPoints.”

Did the Missing Presentation Contain the Underlying Analysis We Had Been Looking For?

And this is where the timeline becomes especially important. Districtwide NIET/TAP had already been approved four months earlier. Now, in August, the administration was presenting what it called its data analysis and root-cause work, and one of the priorities coming out of that discussion was to “invest in teacher capacity.” Yet the presentation the Board was looking at—the material that might show how the district moved from its data to that conclusion—is not available in the public records we have been able to locate.

If the August presentation contained the district’s root-cause analysis, it may contain the missing evidence underlying that decision—the evidence we have been trying to find and analyze.

So Why Can't the Public See It?

That leaves some important questions. Why isn’t the presentation shown in the public workshop video? Why wasn’t it included among the publicly posted workshop materials? And why, when South San AFT specifically requested the PowerPoint referred to in the workshop, did the district respond “There were no PowerPoints”?

So What Did the Board of Managers Review Before Voting?

The public record does not tell us what additional South San-specific evidence, if any, individual Board members reviewed before voting. So we asked them directly.

We Asked the Board of Managers

On September 8, 2026, we sent questions individually to each member of the Board of Managers asking what role they personally played in reviewing the evidence before the April 14 vote, what South San-specific evidence they reviewed before approving districtwide NIET/TAP, whether the promised Needs Assessment or root-cause analysis was completed, and where the public can review that evidence.

As of September 24, 2026, no responses had been received. Check hereto see whether any responses have been received since then. 

Without the underlying presentation, we cannot see what evidence was shown for deficiencies in teaching practice, how strongly that evidence supported the district’s conclusions, or how teacher-related factors compared with other possible causes of low student performance.

Major district decisions should be supported by data and analysis. The public should be able to review the underlying data and analysis—with personal or identifying information removed where necessary—so they can evaluate whether the evidence supports the district’s conclusions.

And NIET/TAP implementation was not a minor change. It involved additional costs, changed how teachers are trained, coached, observed, and evaluated, and shifted more instructional decision-making from individual teachers toward district-directed expectations and practices.

Which brings us back to the question at the center of this series: Were teachers really what South San needed to fix—and did the evidence warrant such a major districtwide restructuring of classroom instruction?

Based on the public record we have been able to review, we still cannot answer that question. The August presentation may contain the data and root-cause analysis that would help answer it, but we cannot evaluate evidence we cannot see. Until that underlying material is available for public review, we cannot independently determine whether the evidence supported making deficiencies in teaching practice such a significant focus of South San’s improvement strategy.

Parents, students, and the broader community need to be able to understand and evaluate major district decisions for themselves.
