Why Did South San Change How Teachers Teach?

Were Teachers Really What South San Needed to Fix? — Part 1

Published: September 2026

A Bit of Background: Why Did TEA Replace South San’s Board?

On February 19, 2025, TEA replaced South San’s elected Board of Trustees with a seven-member Board of Managers after years of documented governance problems, financial-control concerns, and repeated state investigations. TEA’s stated reasons for the intervention centered primarily on longstanding problems with district governance and management. 

Two months later, on April 14, 2025, the newly installed Board of Managers, acting on the superintendent’s recommendation, approved districtwide implementation of NIET/TAP, a school-improvement  model developed outside the district.

What Is NIET/TAP?

TAP (The System for Teacher and Student Advancement) was introduced in 1999 by Lowell Milken, whose professional background was in law, business, and philanthropy rather than classroom teaching. He later founded the National Institute for Excellence in Teaching (NIET).

NIET/TAP uses prescribed instructional practices, professional development, coaching, observation, evaluation, accountability, and performance measures to move classroom practice toward the model’s expectations. NIET describes its rubric as defining effective teaching and creating a common language and vision for instruction. The model also includes performance-based compensation tied to teacher roles, classroom performance, and student performance.

“They would help us establish protocols for PLCs, establish protocols of how to evaluate teachers, establish protocols of how to evaluate master teachers.” — Dr. Saul Hinojosa, April 14, 2025 Board meeting 

But South San Already Has Standards and Teacher Evaluation Systems

South San already operates within common academic standards and an established system for evaluating teachers. The TEKS establish what Texas students are expected to know and be able to do. T-TESS provides a common statewide framework for defining and evaluating effective teaching, and South San also has a Teacher Incentive Allotment system that uses teacher-observation and student-growth measures in determining teacher designations and incentive funding.

Already in place before districtwide NIET/TAP:[Make Bullets] TEKS - what students are expected to learnT-TESS - common framework for evaluating teachingTIA - evaluation system linking teacher observation, student growth, and incentive funding

NIET/TAP therefore does not create academic standards, teacher evaluation, feedback, or performance incentives; those already exist. It adds a more intensive system for defining, coaching, observing, evaluating, and eventually rewarding instructional practice. And unlike South San’s earlier TIA development process, we have not found evidence in the public materials we reviewed that classroom teachers had a comparable role in deciding whether or how NIET/TAP would be adopted districtwide.

How is NIET/TAP changing teaching in South San classrooms?

At South San, the decision changed both how teachers were expected to teach and how their teaching would be monitored and evaluated. Under NIET/TAP, teachers are coached, observed, and evaluated against a detailed instructional rubric. NIET/TAP was also implemented alongside broader district curriculum requirements that affected the materials teachers could use and how much latitude they had to adapt instruction to differences among students.

But common learning expectations do not require identical instruction. Different students, classes, subjects, and circumstances require different explanations, pacing, grouping, materials, or approaches. Variation in instruction can benefit students because it allows teachers to respond to what is actually happening in the classroom rather than following the same approach regardless of student need.

Common learning expectations do not require identical instruction—and greater uniformity in instruction does not, by itself, mean better instruction.

NIET/TAP represents a significant shift in who directs day-to-day instruction. Instead of teachers primarily deciding how best to teach the required standards, instructional practice is increasingly shaped by NIET/TAP’s defined expectations and reinforced through district-directed training, coaching, observation, and evaluation. NIET/TAP may call for teachers to respond to student needs, but it also defines and evaluates what effective instructional practice should look like. That shifts more authority over how teachers respond to students from the classroom teacher toward the system evaluating the teacher.

What Does This Mean for Students?

Much of the real work of education happens in the moments when teachers use their knowledge, experience, creativity, and relationships with students to respond to what a class or an individual student needs. A teacher may need to slow down, explain something differently, abandon a planned activity, provide more practice, challenge students who are ready to move ahead, or take an approach that was not anticipated in advance. When teachers have less freedom to make those judgments, students may also lose some of the flexibility, creativity, individual attention, and responsiveness that can help them learn, stay engaged, and succeed.

Moving from a system in which teachers exercise substantial professional judgment over how to teach their students to one in which district-prescribed expectations increasingly define and monitor instructional practice is a significant shift. It changes not only how teachers are evaluated, but also who controls day-to-day instructional decisions.

The concern is not whether teachers should respond to student needs. It is who decides what that response should look like—the teacher in the classroom, or the system evaluating the teacher?

The issue is not whether teachers should respond to student needs—of course they should. The concern is who decides what that response should look like. Under NIET/TAP, teachers are trained and coached toward defined instructional practices and then observed and evaluated against that same framework. A teacher may be exercising professional judgment in response to students, but if the evaluator determines that the observed practice does not meet the rubric’s expectations, that judgment can result in a lower evaluation score. The system therefore does more than support teachers in responding to students; it also judges whether they responded in the way the framework defines as effective.

So why did South San make such a significant shift in instructional decision-making when academic standards, teacher evaluation, professional development, and teacher supports were already in place? Before restructuring teaching districtwide, was there evidence that deficiencies in teacher practice were serious, widespread, and a significant contributor to low student performance?
